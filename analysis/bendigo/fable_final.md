## The call

**"Winding up an old wind-up alarm clock."**

If the hosts ask for more: "with the key on the back".

### Why this one

1. The mechanism is settled. The clue is a click-pawl ratchet being wound by hand: 13-22 pawl drops per second,
   each drop with a 2-7 ms bounce and a weaker secondary click 8-18 ms later, a clean floor between drops, and the
   grandfather-clock winding reproduces every one of those timing features (rate, double click, secondary click,
   dynamic range). The owner's ear and the matcher (0.82-0.84 on five independent slices) agree. Everything that is
   not a mainspring ratchet (timers, toys, dials, reels, lever pulls, running clockwork) is "way off" by ear, which
   is what the measurements predicted for them.
2. What differs from the grandfather clock is exactly what a small spring clock changes. The clue's ring is a pair at
   2.32/2.55 kHz that dies in 0.1-0.25 s; the floor clock rings at 1.1-1.5 kHz for 0.2-1.9 s. That is a ringing part
   about half the size, damped by being in a tinplate case in someone's hand rather than a brass wheel in a wooden
   cabinet. A twin-bell alarm clock has two 40-55 mm steel bells, nominally identical and never quite the same
   pitch, sitting on the case the ratchet is screwed to: a ready-made explanation for a split pair at 2.3-2.6 kHz
   that no single bell or plain wheel gives. The clue's heavy first hit with a 60-90 Hz thump is the clock being
   picked up or the key pushed in; a floor clock cannot do that. The clue's burst of ~6 fading drops is one half-turn
   flip of a small key, not a crank stroke.
3. The clues all land: "I'm a classic" and "I'm old school" (a wind-up alarm clock is the stock image of both),
   "I need a hand" (a clock's hands, and winding is done by hand). The nearest rejected guess, "clock ticking", shows
   callers already hear a clock; they have guessed the wrong action on the right object.
4. No candidate outside the clock family survives both the ear and the measurements. Playing the spoons and a teaspoon
   in a mug fitted the measurements but not the ratchet cadence the ear now locks onto.

### Generic or specific?

Specific. Secret Sound answer cards name the object and the action ("winding up a ..."), and hosts send a vague answer
back with "you need to be more specific" rather than paying it. "Winding up an old clock" risks that, and it also
sounds like a guess rather than an identification. "Winding up an old wind-up alarm clock" carries the generic
answer inside it (it is winding, and it is a clock) so a judge holding "winding a clock" or "winding an alarm clock"
pays it, and a judge holding "mantel clock" hears the right action and the right family. Do not add detail that could
be wrong (brand, "twin bell", "Big Ben"); "old" and "wind-up" are safe because they are in the clues.

## Ranked alternatives (one per subsequent call)

1. **"Winding up a grandfather clock."** The ear's closest match and a "classic, old school" object with hands; the
   only strike against it is the one good recording's lower, longer ring, which another clock, mic or the station's
   processing could change.
2. **"Winding up an old mantel clock with the key."** Same key-wound ratchet, case bigger than an alarm clock and
   smaller than a floor clock; untested by ear because the libraries have no clean recording.
3. **"Pulling the chain to wind up a cuckoo clock."** A fast hand-pulled click ratchet on a small wooden case, with
   the chain giving the extra chatter; untested by ear.
4. **"Winding up an old carriage clock."** Small brass case, key-wound, rings around 2-3 kHz; untested.
5. **"Playing the spoons."** The best non-clock fit to the measurements (two hand-damped steel bowls, knee thud on
   the heavy stroke, long-short alternation); use only after the clock family is exhausted.

Order of play if calls are limited: alarm clock, grandfather clock, mantel clock, cuckoo clock, carriage clock, spoons.
